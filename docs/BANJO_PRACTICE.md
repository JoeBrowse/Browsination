# Banjo learning plan: what the practice research says

Joe asked whether learning several pieces at once, in small chunks, with the app deciding what to review
and when to switch pieces, is a sensible way to practise. Short answer: yes, with three caveats. This is
the evidence the plan is built on and where it is thin.

## Interleaving across pieces (switching, not blocking)

- Carter & Grahn (2016), ten advanced clarinettists, two pieces practised blocked (12 minutes each) and two
  interleaved (3 minutes a piece, alternating, to the same 12 minutes each). One day later the interleaved
  pieces were rated better, significantly so for the concerto expositions. Most players found interleaving
  more useful but still *preferred* blocked practice: it feels worse while you do it and better the next day.
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4989027/
- Stambaugh (2011), beginner clarinettists over three sessions: no difference at the end of practice between
  blocked and random orders; the contextual-interference literature predicts the random group's advantage
  at delayed retention. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429410385945
- Mathias & Goldman (2025), advanced violinists, compared blocked, random and an *increasing* schedule
  (start blocked, then switch more often). Interleaving impairs the first session and improves retention;
  the increasing schedule is the middle path for new material.
  https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00224294231222801
- Gebrian, *Learn Faster, Perform Better* (Oxford, 2024): a violist-neuroscientist's practical version of the
  same findings: switch between sections and pieces often, expect it to feel harder, judge progress the
  next day. https://interlude.hk/how-neuroscience-helps-musicians-learn-faster-perform-better/

What the plan does with it: reviews alternate pieces (never two blocks on the same piece in a row when
another is available) and new bars go to the piece that has waited longest, so a session naturally moves
between pieces. A brand-new chunk is learned in one blocked burst first, then enters the rotation.

## Spacing over days

- Across days the evidence is good: distributing practice over several days improves later retention, and
  sleep helps accuracy (Simmons and Duke's work on musicians' consolidation:
  https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258156119_Distributed_Practice_and_Procedural_Memory_Consolidation_in_Musicians'_Skill_Learning).
- Within a single session the evidence is weak: Wiseheart, D'Souza & Chae (2017) found no spacing effect
  for short piano sequences with gaps of 0 to 15 minutes, and a 2026 motor-sequence study found little or
  none once circadian effects were controlled. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28800631/
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-52702-5

What the plan does with it: a chunk learned today is first tested tomorrow, then at growing gaps
(3, 7, then roughly ×2.6 up to 60 days) while it holds, and back to tomorrow when it slips. Within a session
nothing is spaced; the session is for interleaving.

## Chunks and error correction

- Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009): among advanced pianists, retention the next day was predicted not by how long
  or how many times they practised but by *how*: locating the exact error, isolating that fragment, varying
  tempo on it, and repeating until it was fixed. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851

What the plan does with it: the unit is a few bars, chosen by Joe at the end of each block ("I learned
4 bars"), and the review rating is about *that chunk*. The app does not prescribe chunk size; it remembers
the piece's usual size as the default.

## Caveats, honestly

1. Interleaving is uncomfortable and looks slower inside the session. The ratings that matter are the
   next-day ones, which is why the plan asks for them.
2. The spacing intervals are borrowed from declarative-memory schedulers (SM-2 style). They are a sensible
   shape for motor skills, not a measured one. Ratings drive them, so a chunk that keeps slipping is seen
   more often regardless.
3. All of the music studies are small (10 to 41 players) and mostly on wind and string players. Nothing here
   is banjo-specific.
